Research

My research program focuses on the dynamics of star clusters, particularly old globular clusters. My group uses a combination of kinematic, photometric data, and pulsar timing data, as well as dynamical models and statistical techniques to probe the formation and evolution of these systems (Dickson, VHB, et al. 2026a; Dickson, VHB, et al. 2026b; Fronimos Pouliasis et al. 2026), the stellar remnants they contain (including stellar-mass and intermediate-mass black holes) (Hénault-Brunet et al. 2019; Hénault-Brunet et al. 2020; Dickson, Smith, VHB, et al. 2024; Smith, VHB, et al. 2024), and their stellar initial mass function (Baumgardt, VHB, et al. 2023; Dickson, VHB, et al. 2023).

Recent highlights

How dense were globular clusters when they formed? [Dickson, VHB, et al. 2026a]

By coupling rapid cluster evolution models with multimass equilibrium models, we built a fast framework that fits present-day observations — number density profiles, proper motions, line-of-sight velocities and stellar mass functions — and can infer what a cluster looked like at birth. We validated the method against a large grid of star-by-star Monte Carlo simulations and applied it to a sample of 40 Milky Way clusters, finding initial half-mass densities with a median of 106.4±0.9 M☉ pc−3, denser than any young massive cluster forming in the Local Universe today, but in line with the compact proto-globular clusters now being found at high redshift. The same fits point to bottom-light stellar initial mass functions and relatively small present-day black hole mass fractions (≲ 1.5%).

How much does black hole physics change that answer? [Dickson, VHB, et al. 2026b]

The inferred birth conditions above rest on uncertain assumptions about how black holes form and how many a cluster keeps, so we refitted the same sample under a range of prescriptions for stellar evolution, black hole initial–final mass relations and supernova natal kicks. We can reproduce the same present-day clusters with different black hole prescriptions, but for prescriptions leading to clusters being born with more black holes, higher initial densities are required. For some prescriptions (the SEVN models), this degeneracy increases the inferred initial densities by nearly an order of magnitude (a median of 107.2±1.1 M☉ pc−3). The need for bottom-light mass functions and small present-day black hole fractions, however, holds up across every prescription tested.

CASTOR's view of Milky Way globular clusters and streams

I am interested in how data from upcoming and future instruments/facilities can further shed light on globular clusters and their interaction with the Milky Way. I recently led the Near-Field Cosmology Science Working Group for the Phase 0 study of the Canadian-led Cosmological Advanced Survey Telescope for Optical and uv Research (CASTOR) mission. CASTOR is a proposed wide-field space telescope that would image the skies at ultraviolet (UV) and blue-optical wavelengths. As an example, the figure on the right (from Côté et al. 2025) shows our simulated CASTOR observations of the tidal stream of the globular cluster Palomar 5 (at ~20 kpc): selecting stars first on their CASTOR proper motions, then along the stream’s UV–optical colour–magnitude diagram lifts the stream cleanly out of the Milky Way foreground. Reaching fainter stars than Gaia with good proper-motion precision at these distances (as we showed in Cheng et al. 2024), CASTOR would give a sharper view of the morphology of substructures around globular clusters and tidal streams.

Other research interests

Multiple populations in globular clusters

I have previously worked on the problem of multiple stellar populations in globular clusters. The origin of chemically distinct populations of stars in these ancient systems is still heavily debated. My efforts, using both observations and N-body simulations, have set the stage for the use of stellar kinematics as a probe of formation scenarios for multiple populations (Hénault-Brunet et al. 2015; Cordero, Hénault-Brunet, et al. 2017).

Kinematics of young massive clusters

During my PhD, I used fibre-fed and IFU multi-object spectroscopic observations to study the kinematics of massive stars in the well-known young massive cluster R136. This work showed that this cluster has a low velocity dispersion, suggesting that it is bound and has not significantly suffered from primordial gas expulsion (Hénault-Brunet et al. 2012b). We also found evidence for rotation of this young cluster, indicating that star clusters can form with a large fraction of their kinetic energy in rotation (Hénault-Brunet et al. 2012c). This dataset was also used to improve methods to constrain the velocity dispersion of systems in which radial velocity measurements are polluted by the orbital motions of binary stars (Cottaar & Hénault-Brunet 2014).

Massive stars and binaries

I have been part of several large spectroscopic campaigns targeting massive stars. I was a member of the VLT-FLAMES Tarantula Survey (PI: C.J. Evans) and its follow-up Tarantula Massive Binary Monitoring (TMBM) programme (PI: H. Sana), from which we reported evidence for two distinct populations of massive runaway stars in 30 Doradus (Sana, Ramírez-Agudelo, VHB, et al. 2022). I am a member of the BLOeM campaign (another ESO Large Programme; PI: T. Shenar) monitoring ~1000 massive stars in the low-metallicity conditions of the Small Magellanic Cloud. These surveys indicate that most massive stars are born in binary systems, across a wide range of metallicities (Sana et al. 2013; Sana et al. 2025).

I have worked on interesting individual systems over the years, such as the Be X-ray binary SXP 1062 which we discovered along with its associated supernova remnant (Hénault-Brunet et al. 2012a) and the variable Wolf-Rayet star WR46 (Hénault-Brunet et al. 2011).
